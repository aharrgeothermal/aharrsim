Twenty geothermal development types, simulated

The coaxial closed-loop simulator was extended to run every development type in Khodayar & Björnsson's classification, each extension was benchmarked against a public reference, and one representative example of each type was run for its design life. This report records the code changes, the benchmarks, the cases, the produced water temperature over time and the electricity it allows.

What was done. Three well configurations were added to the solver (single-channel production and injection wells with a feed zone, pump and flashing riser; a U-tube borehole exchanger; a reservoir element in series at the toe of a coaxial well), together with a chaining layer for injector → laterals → producer systems, five reservoir models and a set of surface-plant models. Each was checked against an independent reference: exact steady solutions, Ramey, measured flowing two-phase wells, pygfunction, the GeoCLUSTER database, closed-form reservoir limits and the GEOPHIRES plant correlations. One example per type was then run with the parameters of the source table's named examples, and the produced water was passed through the plant suited to it under one set of reference conditions (15 °C ambient, 40 °C condenser, 70 % pumps).

What came out. The produced water temperature sorts the twenty types into four rungs. Below about 80 °C (hot sedimentary doublets, abandoned-well and short closed loops, the heat-pump sources) there is heat and no electricity. From 80 to 120 °C a binary plant converts 10–20 % of a small exergy: co-produced oil-field water, surface waste heat, the cyclic fractures. From 120 to 200 °C electricity is the product: pumped medium-temperature wells, EGS doublets, the long multilateral loop. Above 200 °C flash and steam plants take over: the Wairakei and Geysers wells, the superhot EGS doublet and the supercritical well, which gives 13.4 MWe from 20 kg/s. What sets the scale is where the heat comes from: anything that sweeps a convecting or fractured reservoir reaches megawatts per well; anything that depends on conduction through a borehole wall stays at about a megawatt or less, however deep the hole. The conventional and EGS capacities quoted in the source table agree with the model; the closed-loop claims do not.

Code changes (v1.2 → v2.0)

The 1.2 solver handled one object: a coaxial loop with a feed stream and a riser, solved monolithically with the rock. Version 2.0 keeps that core and adds the configurations below without a second code path for the rock: the same axisymmetric grid, the same BDF2 time stepping and the same monolithic fluid–rock solve serve every mode. The additions are in the fluid part (what flows where, and what it is coupled to at the ends) and in two new modules for what happens beyond the well.

What changed in the solver

  • Well modes. mode: production and injection run one flow channel (the casing, or a tubing with a static annulus treated as a conducting layer). A production well is fed at TD by temperature or enthalpy; the feed enthalpy is evaluated at reservoir pressure (pres − ṁ/PI, and at least 1.02 psat) so a liquid feed stays liquid until the riser lets it flash. The boundary condition is a wellhead pressure or a bottom pressure. A downhole pump splits the pressure march at the pump depth: the flowing bottom-hole pressure pwf = pres − ṁ/PI is marched up to the pump, the wellhead pressure is marched down to it, and the difference is the pump head; a suction pressure floor of 1 bar flags a pump set above the fluid level. Two-phase flow in the riser uses the 1.2 drift-flux model unchanged. The fluid heat-storage term is off for single channels (steady-flow fluid energy), which removes a stiff term that added nothing at the time scales of interest.
  • U-tube. mode: utube couples both legs to the borehole wall through the Hellström delta circuit built from the multipole borehole resistance (Rb) and the internal resistance (Ra): leg-to-wall Rlw = 2Rb, leg-to-leg Rll = 4RbRa/(4Rb − Ra). The rock grid starts at the borehole wall.
  • Far field. far_field: adiabatic makes the outer radial boundary a symmetry plane, so a well in an array or a lateral in a stack is a unit cell of radius rfar.
  • Reservoir element at TD. bottom_element places any object with T_out(T_in, t) in series at the toe of a coaxial well: the feed stream's enthalpy is raised by the element before it enters the riser, and the heat added, Qres, enters the energy balance.
  • Energy balance. Every record now closes Qwellhead = Qrock + Qres − dEfluid − Qke − Qpe, with the potential-energy term Qpe = ṁ g TVD (positive for production, negative for injection, zero for a loop); the residual is reported per run and is at round-off for every case in this report.
  • Safeguards for hot wells. A production well starts from the flowing state (warm start) rather than a static column; the enthalpy change per iteration is capped at 300 kJ/kg; under-relaxation (ω from 0.6, halved on a reversal, floor 0.25) is switched on while steam is present; enthalpies are clipped at the 1 °C liquid value. These fixed the stalls met on the Wairakei and IDDP cases.
  • Input. New YAML keys mode, inflow (T_C | T_K | h_J_kg, p_res_MPa, PI_kg_s_per_MPa), pump (md_m, efficiency), utube (pipe_id/pipe_od/shank_spacing in m, mm or in, k_pipe, k_grout), pressure_bc.bottom_MPa, far_field; tubing is optional; radii accept _mm. Validation rejects inconsistent combinations (a pump without a productivity index, a bottom element on anything but an annulus-down coaxial well).

Benchmarks of the extensions

Each extension was checked against an independent public reference before the cases were run. The checks are independent in kind: an exact solution for the discretisation, a classical analytical model for the transient, measured wells for the two-phase hydraulics, a reference implementation for the U-tube and the chains, closed-form limits for the reservoir elements and the Aspen-derived GEOPHIRES correlations for the plants.

Cases and results, type by type

Each card gives the type as the source table describes it, the example that was run, how it was modelled, the produced water temperature and the heat and electricity over the design life, and (where a well was solved) the temperature profile along the well at the end of the run. Reference conditions throughout: 15 °C ambient; GEOPHIRES practical binary (air-cooled, brine returned at its reinjection temperature) for liquid water; single or double flash or a condensing steam turbine for two-phase and steam streams, 40 °C condenser; 70 % pumps. "Gross" is the plant output; "pumps" is downhole lift plus circulation against the fracture impedance; heat is relative to each case's return temperature.

Discussion

What the water temperature allows, and what sets it

The produced water temperature decides the conversion route, and two things decide the temperature: how hot the rock is where the fluid takes its heat, and whether the fluid sweeps that rock or only touches it through a borehole wall. The first is geology and depth; the second is the development type. Every type that sweeps a volume (an aquifer, a convective reservoir, a fracture network) delivers water within a few kelvin of the rock temperature for as long as the swept volume lasts. Every type that relies on conduction into a hole (the shallow exchangers, the single-well retrofits, the multilateral and ultradeep loops) delivers water far below the rock temperature from the first day, because a cooled borehole can only pass a few hundred watts per metre: for rock of 3 W/m/K after ten years the line-source result is about 1.7 W/m per kelvin of driving difference, so 253 W/m at 150 K. Three kilometres of hole in a 255 °C field (type 7) therefore give 0.66 MWth and 56 °C water at 10 kg/s; sixty-nine kilometres in a 263 °C field (type 8) give 30 MWth at 111 °C; twenty kilometres to 500 °C (type 20) give 8.9 MWth at 146 °C. The numbers scale with metres of hot hole, not with depth or rock temperature alone.

The ladder follows. Below about 80 °C the exergy is too small for any plant: the Paris-type doublet (63 °C, 6.5 MWth), the abandoned well (39 °C, 0.19 MWth), the short closed doublet (52 °C, 1.8 MWth), the GreenLoop retrofit (56 °C) and the Eden-type single well after its small fracture volume has cooled (133 → 57 °C) are heat sources for networks, buildings, greenhouses and absorption chillers; the two shallow exchangers (29 and 27 W/m, COP about 3.5) are heat-pump sources and consume electricity. Between 80 and 120 °C a binary plant converts 10–20 % of the exergy, which is itself a small fraction of the heat: 0.56 MWe from the 8.4 MWth waste-heat stream, 0.72 MWe from eight co-producing oil wells at 93 °C, 0.10 MWe from twenty cyclic fractures at 72 °C. From 120 to 200 °C electricity becomes the product: the pumped Nevada-type well (1.8 MWe net at 147 °C), the Soultz-type doublet (1.4 MWe net at 185 °C), the Fervo-type doublet in its early years (3.6 MWe gross at 184 °C) and the Eavor-type loop (1.2 MWe at 111 °C). Above 200 °C flash and steam plants take over and the output per well jumps: 4.5 MWe from the Wairakei-type well, 7.3 MWe from the Geysers-type steam well, 5.9 MWe (after 30 years) from the superhot doublet and 13.4 MWe from the supercritical well.

Conventional types (1, 2, 3, 3b)

These are the types with decades of field experience, and the model lands where the fields do. The Paris-type doublet gives 6.5 MWth of heat; its thermal breakthrough at 8.3 years is early because the example aquifer is thin (20 m) and the wells 1.2 km apart; the Dogger doublets of the 1980s with 1–1.5 km spacing in thicker producing intervals have run 30–40 years with a few kelvin of cooling, which is what the Gringarten–Sauty model gives when the thickness is doubled. The Nevada-type well makes 2.2 MWe gross from 75 kg/s at 15 °C ambient and spends 0.40 MWe on its line-shaft pump; the source table's 2–6 MWe per well corresponds to 75–150 kg/s, which is the range of pumped Great Basin wells. The Wairakei-type well gives 4.5 MWe from 40 kg/s of 250 °C fluid flashed twice; Wairakei and similar fields report 4–10 MWe per well at 40–100 kg/s. The dry-steam well gives 7.3 MWe from 15 kg/s, close to 0.5 MWe per kg/s, which is the rule of thumb for The Geysers. The pumped medium-temperature case shows the parasitic that matters in this class: a line-shaft or submersible pump at a few hundred metres takes 10–20 % of the gross output, more as the reservoir pressure declines.

Surface waste heat (4)

With no wells there is nothing to simulate but the plant. The GEOPHIRES correlation and the cycle model agree that a 110 °C stream of 50 kg/s cooled to 70 °C yields 0.56 MWe at 15 °C ambient, 6.7 % of the heat. That is the right order for the modular units on separated brine at Icelandic and Japanese plants; it falls by a third at 35 °C ambient. Such modules are best read as an efficiency measure on an existing plant rather than a resource in themselves.

Shallow closed loops (5, 6)

The U-tube settles at 29 W/m in its tenth heating season and the 400 m coaxial exchanger at 27 W/m, both inside the design ranges used in practice (20–50 W/m for U-tubes in saturated ground, 20–40 W/m for deep borehole exchangers). The coaxial exchanger with an uninsulated inner pipe loses part of its gain to the short circuit between the up and down streams, which the profile chart shows as the two streams converging near the top. These types turn electricity into three and a half times as much heat; they do not make any.

Deep closed loops (7, 8, 9, 16, 20)

Here the model and the developers' figures part company, and the physics says why. The GreenLoop retrofit gives 56 °C and 0.66 MWth from a 3 km well in a 255 °C field; the 2–9 MWe quoted for the type cannot come from conduction. GreenFire's own Coso demonstration sat in a steam zone, where condensing steam on the outside of the casing supplies heat convectively at a rate conduction cannot; that is a different boundary condition, which the model can represent with a fixed wall temperature but which exists only where a producing steam field already exists. The Eavor-type loop gives 2.3 MWe in year 1 and 1.2 MWe in year 30 from 69 km of hole, which is the independent estimate quoted in the source table (2.2 MWe) and a quarter to a half of the company's 8.2 MWe; the 80 m lateral spacing costs about 5 K by year 30 against isolated laterals (116 °C against 111 °C), so spacing is a second-order lever and length of hot hole the first. The 20 km Quaise-type loop gives 171 → 146 °C and 1.0 → 0.59 MWe at 20 kg/s; doubling the flow lowers the water to 107 °C and the output to 0.40 MWe, the signature of a conduction-starved exchanger. The 100 MWe quoted for the type is a plant of many wells producing supercritical fluid from an open system, a different type from the closed loop the source table files it under. The abandoned-well loop (39 °C, 0.19 MWth) is a building-heat source consistent with the 400 kW trial quoted. In every one of these the heat comes at the price of a lower temperature than the rock, so the exergy, and with it the electricity, is a small fraction of an already small heat flow.

EGS (10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 19)

Fractured rock is swept rock, and the model gives EGS the temperatures of the rock for as long as the fractured volume lasts. The Soultz-type doublet holds 200 °C at the fracture face for 30 years because its twenty fractures 50 m apart over a 600 m path hold about 140 years of heat at 30 kg/s; the net 1.4 MWe after circulation against the impedance is in line with Soultz's 1.7 MWe gross plant and United Downs' 3 MWe plan at higher flow. The Fervo-type doublet reproduces the Project Red pilot in year 1 (3.6 MWe gross at 184 °C for 63 L/s) and then shows what a pilot geometry cannot do: with laterals 100 m apart along 1 km, the rock between them is swept out in about ten years (79 °C at the fracture face by year 10, 64 °C water at year 30) whatever the number of fractures, because the fracture count sets how early the decline starts and the rock volume per kilogram per second sets how long the heat lasts. A 30-year commercial design needs several times that volume per well pair: longer laterals, wider spacing, more wells, or replacement wells, which is the direction of Fervo's later designs. The Eden-type single well has only a 150 m path through three fractures; it cools from 133 to 57 °C in 30 years and is a heat source. The superhot doublet starts at 11.9 MWe gross and ends at 5.9 MWe (5.0 net) as its block cools from 375 to 285 °C; superhot rock multiplies the exergy per kilogram, but the heat in place per fracture is no larger than in ordinary EGS, so the design needs the same large fracture networks and brings the chemistry of supercritical fluids with it.

The cyclic single-fracture systems are a special case: between cycles they are conduction systems. A 12 h soak reaches a diffusion length of about 0.2 m into the rock on either side of the fracture, so each day's heat is what that shell holds: about 7 MWh per cycle for a 300 × 100 m fracture in 150 °C rock, falling to a settled 72 °C after a year of cycles. Twenty such wells give 5.9 MWth and 0.10 MWe against the 50 MWe quoted; a fracture ten times larger in 200 °C rock would raise the output by roughly an order of magnitude, still well short. The storage test gives the same lesson from the other side: of the heat charged into the fracture at 150 °C, 15 % comes back in the next discharge and 0.5 % as electricity, because the heat conducts into the rock during the soak and returns slowly over later cycles; the fracture is a good heat sink and a poor battery.

Oil and gas assets (15, 16, 17)

Co-produced water is the nearest of these to an existing operation: eight wells lifting 15 kg/s each of 95 °C water give 16.5 MWth and 0.72 MWe, about 90 kWe per well, which brackets the 20–350 kWe per field quoted (the example is on the warm side; many fields produce 60–80 °C water). The heat is free in the sense that the lifting belongs to oil production; the electricity is small because the exergy of 93 °C water is small. The SAGD chamber is a one-off store: 0.24 MWe at 112 °C in year 1 and nothing after year 5, with 39 % of the heat in place recovered in 15 years, almost all of it in the first five. It is worth a short-lived heat supply to the facility, not a power plant. The abandoned-well loop is discussed with the closed loops above.

Supercritical (18)

The IDDP-type well is the largest single-well output in the set, 13.4 MWe and 49 MWth from 20 kg/s, because the fluid arrives at the wellhead as steam at 311 °C and 10 MPa: 0.67 MWe per kg/s, against 0.1 for the Wairakei-type flash well and 0.03 for the pumped binary well. The >30 MWe expected of such a well needs about 50 kg/s, which is what IDDP-1 flowed before its chemistry (HCl, silica) and materials problems closed it. The model carries none of that chemistry; it shows the thermodynamic prize and the flow needed to collect it.

Across the set

  • Agreement with the source table. The conventional and EGS capacities quoted there are consistent with the model (type 2 at higher flow, types 3, 3b, 10, 14 in its early years, 18 at IDDP-1's flow). The closed-loop claims are not: 2–9 MWe per retrofitted well, 8.2 MWe per multilateral loop and 100 MWe per 20 km plant sit three to ten times above what conduction supplies, and the 50 MWe for twenty cyclic wells sits two orders above it. The one independent closed-loop figure in the table (2.2 MWe for the Eavor loop) is what the model gives.
  • Decline. Convective reservoirs and large fracture networks hold their temperature for 30 years; small fractured volumes (11, 14, 19) and the heat store do not; conduction loops decline slowly but start low. The decline chart separates the three behaviours.
  • Parasitics. Pumped medium-temperature wells spend 10–20 % of gross on lift; EGS doublets 0.5–1 MWe on circulation against the impedance (a quarter of gross at Soultz-type flows); self-flowing high-temperature wells and thermosiphoning deep loops spend almost nothing. The Fervo-type doublet in year 1 spends 1.4 MWe of its 3.6 on the ESP and the impedance.
  • Climate. All figures are for 15 °C ambient. With the GEOPHIRES availability at the actual ambient, the binary outputs fall by 16–31 % at 25 °C, 30–55 % at 35 °C and 42–72 % at 45 °C, the cooler streams losing most; raising the condenser from 40 to 50 °C costs the flash and steam plants 5–11 %. Direct heat is unaffected. In a Gulf summer the binary cases are roughly half of what is shown, the flash and steam cases nine-tenths, and the heat cases unchanged.
  • Sensitivities not run here. Rock conductivity ±20 % moves the closed-loop heat by about ±15 %; fracture count and area move the EGS decline time in proportion to the rock volume they enclose; the productivity index and reservoir pressure move the pump power and whether a well self-flows; the GEOPHIRES binary correlation and the cycle model differ by up to 11 %, which is the uncertainty to carry on the binary figures; the flash model is within 3 % of GEOPHIRES at the same condenser temperature.
  • What the model does not contain. Reservoir pressure decline and reinjection returns, scaling and corrosion, the chemistry of supercritical fluids, the mechanics of fracture creation and the number of flow paths an EGS actually makes, non-condensable gases, and temperature-dependent rock conductivity. Fracture counts, areas and spacings are inputs; two examples (9 and 12/13) use assumed geometries because none is published.

What it means for a screening decision

Measured against the electricity-price screen kept alongside this study (score zero at IRENA's geothermal LCOE of 0.056–0.060 USD/kWh, which is conventional flash and binary economics at several megawatts per well), only the types that sweep a hot reservoir reach the per-well scale that LCOE rests on: the high-temperature flash and steam wells, pumped medium-temperature wells at 75–150 kg/s, EGS doublets with large fracture networks, and supercritical wells if their chemistry can be managed. The closed-loop and single-well cyclic types deliver a megawatt or less of electricity per well whatever the depth, so their cost per kilowatt-hour sits far above that LCOE; in a market priced at 0.08–0.11 USD/kWh they are heat projects (networks, absorption cooling, desalination pre-heat, process heat) or avoided-diesel cases, not grid power. The two questions to ask of any proposal in this classification are therefore: does the fluid sweep the rock or only touch it, and how many years of heat does the swept volume hold at the proposed flow.
