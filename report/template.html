Deep Coaxial Loop Simulator

How much heat a 4–12 km closed-loop coaxial well delivers to surface, and the evidence that the simulator computing it is right. Cold water is pumped down the annulus, returns hot up an insulated centre tubing, and gains heat from the rock by conduction only. The working fluid is water and steam (IAPWS-95).

Heat delivered to surface, 4–12 km

The reference loop is the same at every depth: 9⅝″ casing to 3 km, a 7″ liner to TD, and 3½″ × 4½″ vacuum-insulated tubing to the bottom, with an apparent conductivity of 0.03 W/m/K across the insulation annulus (0.71 K·m/W per metre of tubing). The rock has k = 2.8 W/m/K and the geotherm is 30 °C/km from 15 °C, so the bottom-hole rock temperature runs from 135 °C at 4 km to 375 °C at 12 km. Water is injected at 40 °C and delivered at 5 MPa, which keeps it single-phase.

Heat grows faster than depth: each extra kilometre adds hotter rock and more contact area at the same time. At 10 kg/s, an 8 km loop gives {{q8}} MWth after 10 years and a 12 km loop {{q12}} MWth ({{cum8}} and {{cum12}} GWh over 30 years). Doubling the flow from 10 to 20 kg/s adds only {{dbl_lo}}–{{dbl_hi}} % more heat, because the rock near the well, not the water, limits the supply. It lowers the outlet temperature by {{dbl_dT}} K at 12 km and multiplies the pumping power in 3½″ tubing: at 12 km, 20 kg/s gives {{q12_20}} MWth for {{pump12_20}} kW of pumping.

Where the heat comes from

The profiles below show the undisturbed rock, the cold stream going down the annulus and the hot stream coming up the tubing after 10 years. The annulus only starts to gain heat once the rock is hotter than the injected water; the deepest third of the well supplies {{bot3}} of the heat. On the way up, the tubing passes {{loss12}} MW (at 12 km) back through the insulation to the colder annulus. That heat stays in the loop, but it lowers the outlet temperature and pre-warms the down-going water, so the rock gives up less. The tubing insulation is therefore one of the main design levers (next section).

Sensitivities

All at 10 kg/s and 10 years; one input changed at a time from the reference design. The geothermal gradient and the tubing insulation dominate. Rock conductivity matters less than either. Gradients were run from 20 to 60 °C/km in 5 °C/km steps (every other one is plotted; the 5 GW section has them all). Leaving the lower half of the tubing uninsulated is the costliest simplification: the up-flowing water gives its heat straight back to the annulus. With plain steel tubing over the full length almost no heat reaches surface, because the two streams simply exchange heat with each other.

Larger completion for higher flow

With 9⅝″ casing to TD and 4½″ × 5½″ insulated tubing, 20–40 kg/s becomes hydraulically practical. The extra flow mostly buys a lower outlet temperature for a modest gain in heat, because the rock around the well, not the water, limits the supply.

{{T_bigbore}}

Producing up the annulus

The loop can also be run the other way: inject down the insulated tubing and produce up the annulus, as in an oil well. Seventeen casing-and-tubing combinations were run both ways (7″ to 13⅜″ casing, 2⅜″ to 7″ vacuum-insulated tubing), which takes the annulus velocity at 10 kg/s from {{dir_vhi}} m/s down to {{dir_vlo}} m/s. Producing up the annulus delivers {{dir_lo}}–{{dir_hi}} % less heat than the reference arrangement in the same completion, with the outlet {{dir_dT}} K cooler, and about half the net power. The penalty is larger at low flow ({{dir_r5}} % at 5 kg/s) and smaller at high flow ({{dir_r20}} % at 20 kg/s), and about the same at 60 °C/km ({{dir_g60}} %).

The reason is the direction of flow relative to the geotherm. Going down the annulus, the water meets hotter rock as it warms, so the temperature difference driving the heat is kept up all the way to TD, and the hot return is insulated from the rock. Going up the annulus, the water meets cooler rock as it warms: it peaks at about {{dir_peak}} °C around {{dir_peakz}} km, then gives {{dir_loss}} MW back to the rock above {{dir_xo}} km, where the rock is colder than the water. The profiles below show both.

Slowing the annulus flow does not help: over the twentyfold range of velocity the heat produced up the annulus only varies between {{dir_qlo}} and {{dir_qhi}} MWth, and that variation follows the casing size and the tubing insulation, not the velocity. The film at the casing wall is well under 1 % of the thermal resistance between the water and the rock, so the velocity has no leverage on the heat; at a given mass flow the water carries the same heat per kilogram however wide the channel. A larger hole adds a little rock contact ({{dir_big_gain}} % from 9⅝″ to 13⅜″), and a larger insulated tubing of the same construction leaks more between the streams (7″ in 13⅜″ gives {{dir_q7}} MWth; with the 3½″ tubing's wall resistance it gives {{dir_r7}}). What the annulus area does change is pumping: the tight annuli (clearance under an inch) cost {{dir_pump_tight}} kW, while from 9⅝″ casing upwards the loop drives itself at 10 kg/s in either direction. With bare steel tubing both directions deliver nothing, because the two streams exchange heat with each other instead.

Delivering steam

With a 30 °C/km geotherm a conduction-only loop delivers pressurised hot water, not steam. After 10 years the wellhead water is at 80–160 °C. Flashed to 0.5 MPa it gives at most {{st_x05}} % steam by mass (12 km, 5 kg/s); flashed to atmospheric pressure, {{st_atm_lo}}–{{st_atm_hi}} %. Useful steam needs hotter rock, lower flow or a deeper well, and lower flow means less total heat. At 50–60 °C/km the 10–12 km loops deliver {{st_hotT}} °C water at 10 kg/s, which flashes to {{st_hot}} % steam at 0.5 MPa. For most designs a binary (ORC) or direct-use plant fed with the hot water will use the heat better than flashing it.

At 5 kg/s the hot riser outweighs the cold annulus by more than the friction, so the loop circulates without a pump: the inlet pressure is only {{st_pin5}} MPa at 8, 10 and 12 km for 5 MPa at the wellhead. Where the delivery pressure is lower still, the loop has to be throttled; the model then holds the inlet at 0.2 MPa and reports the pressure taken by the wellhead choke. At 10 kg/s the pump must add {{st_pump10}} MPa. The model computes flashing inside the tubing when the wellhead pressure is low enough, but in these designs the riser stays single-phase.

Wells for a 5 GW field

The number of loops needed for 5 GW, by depth and geothermal gradient. Each count holds 5 GW through year 30; sizing on first-year output needs about a fifth fewer wells, but output then falls by about a fifth over the 30 years. Heat uses the reference design at 10 kg/s. Power uses whichever of 5, 10, 15 and 20 kg/s gives the most net electricity: lower flow means hotter water, which matters more for power than the extra heat. The conversion follows GeoCLUSTER’s ORC method (20 % of the thermal exergy at 100 °C rising to 45 % at 200 °C, nothing below 100 °C), with a 20 °C heat sink and the circulation pump deducted.

At 30 °C/km a 5 GWth field needs {{s5_12_30}} wells at 12 km and {{s5_4_30}} at 4 km; at 60 °C/km, {{s5_12_60}} and {{s5_4_60}}. Power takes far more: {{s5e_12_30}} wells at 12 km and 30 °C/km, {{s5e_12_60}} at 60 °C/km. The gradients span surface heat flows of 56–168 mW/m² at k = 2.8 W/m/K, against a continental average of about 65 mW/m², so the upper half of the range applies only to volcanic or rifting areas. Above about 400 °C at TD (marked †) granite turns ductile and current casing, cement and insulated tubing are beyond their ratings; at 12 km and 60 °C/km the rock at TD is 735 °C, close to where wet granite starts to melt. Those cases show what conduction could supply, not what can be built.

Best case (“high-high”)

Every input at the most favourable value tested: 60 °C/km, rock k 3.5 W/m/K, the large-bore completion with insulation k 0.01 W/m/K, and 5–40 kg/s. At 12 km one well gives {{mx_q_12}} MWth or {{mx_p_12}} MWe, so 5 GW takes {{mx_th_12}} wells for heat or {{mx_e_12}} for power. It is an upper bound, not a design: k 3.5 W/m/K with 60 °C/km implies 210 mW/m² of heat flow, and the deeper cases are well past the 400 °C line.

{{T_max}}

Rock conductivity falling with temperature

The study holds rock conductivity constant. Letting it fall with temperature, as it does in crystalline rock, cuts the heat per well by {{kT30}} % at 30 °C/km and {{kT60}} % at 60 °C/km (shallow to deep), and raises the well counts in proportion. The hottest cases are therefore the most optimistic.

{{T_kT}}

Spacing: after 30 years the cooled rock reaches about {{sp_reach}} m from each well. On a 100 m grid, neighbouring wells cut each other’s output by about {{sp100}} % ({{sp100m}} % in the best case, {{sp150m}} % at 150 m), so a field needs roughly 0.01–0.02 km² per well at depth. The twenty development types

Khodayar and Björnsson (2024) sort geothermal developments into twenty types, from hot sedimentary aquifers and volcanic flash fields to heat pumps, closed loops, fractured-rock EGS, oil-field heat and the supercritical and 20 km concepts. Only three of them are coaxial loops. To run the rest, the solver gained single-channel production and injection wells (feed zone, flashing riser, downhole pump, productivity index), a U-tube borehole exchanger, an adiabatic unit-cell far field, a reservoir element in series at TD, chains of segments with transient hand-over, reservoir models (convective reservoir, Gringarten–Sauty doublet breakthrough, Gringarten parallel-fracture EGS, cyclic fracture, lumped heat store) and surface plants (ORC cycle and the GEOPHIRES practical correlations, single and double flash, steam turbine, heat pump). Each extension was checked against an independent public reference before use:

One representative example of each type was then run for 30 years (10 for the heat pumps, 15 for the heat store, a year of daily cycles for the batch systems), with the parameters taken from the ranges and named examples in the source table. The water temperature is what the model is asked for first; the electricity follows from it. All cases use the same reference conditions: 15 °C ambient, a practical binary plant (GEOPHIRES correlation, air-cooled, brine returned at its reinjection temperature) for liquid water, single or double flash or a condensing steam turbine for two-phase and steam, a 40 °C condenser, 70 % pumps.

What the water temperature allows

Below about 80 °C there is no electricity. The hot sedimentary aquifer doublet ({{dt_T1}} °C, {{dt_H1}} MWth), the 3 km abandoned-well loop ({{dt_T16}} °C, {{dt_H16}} MWth), the single-well Eden-type EGS ({{dt_T11f}} °C in year 1, {{dt_T11}} °C after 30 years: a small fractured volume cools quickly), the assumed Chevron-type closed doublet ({{dt_T9}} °C) and the 3 km GreenLoop retrofit even in 255 °C rock ({{dt_T7}} °C at 10 kg/s, {{dt_H7}} MWth) deliver heat for networks, buildings and greenhouses. The two shallow systems are heat-pump sources: {{dt_wm5}} W/m from the 150 m U-tube and {{dt_wm6}} W/m from the 400 m coaxial exchanger, lifted to 45 °C at a COP of about {{dt_cop5}}; they use electricity rather than make it. From 80 to 120 °C a binary plant converts 10–20 % of the small exergy: the eight co-produced oil wells give {{dt_P15}} MWe net at {{dt_T15}} °C, the waste-heat module {{dt_P4}} MWe from 50 kg/s at 110 °C, the SAGD heat store {{dt_P17f}} MWe at {{dt_T17f}} °C in its first year but nothing after year five ({{dt_T17}} °C; the chamber holds a few years of heat, not decades), and the cyclic single-well fracture {{dt_T12}} °C water in daily batches, which twenty phased wells turn into {{dt_P13}} MWe. From 120 to 200 °C electricity becomes the product: the pumped Nevada-type well gives {{dt_P2}} MWe net at {{dt_T2}} °C, the classic EGS doublet {{dt_P10}} MWe at {{dt_T10}} °C after 30 years, the Fervo-type horizontal doublet {{dt_P14f}} MWe gross at {{dt_T14f}} °C in year 1, the same as the 3.5 MWe pilot, but nothing after about year twelve ({{dt_T14}} °C at 30 years), because the rock between two laterals 100 m apart is swept out in about ten years whatever the number of fractures (a 30-year plant needs several times that rock volume per well pair, or replacement wells), and the Eavor-type loop with 60 km of laterals {{dt_P8}} MWe from {{dt_H8}} MWth at {{dt_T8}} °C ({{dt_P8f}} MWe at {{dt_T8f}} °C in year 1). Above 200 °C flash and steam plants take over: the Wairakei-type well {{dt_P3}} MWe at {{dt_T3}} °C wellhead temperature, the Geysers-type dry-steam well {{dt_P3b}} MWe, the IDDP-type supercritical well {{dt_P18}} MWe from 20 kg/s of steam at {{dt_T18}} °C and 10 MPa (0.67 MWe per kg/s, so the 30 MWe expected of such a well needs about 50 kg/s), the superhot EGS doublet {{dt_P19f}} MWe in year 1 ({{dt_T19}} °C at the 5 MPa wellhead, steam fraction {{dt_x19f}}) falling to {{dt_P19}} MWe after 30 years as the fractured block cools from 375 to {{dt_Tres19}} °C (steam fraction {{dt_x19}}), and the 20 km Quaise-type loop {{dt_P20}} MWe at {{dt_T20}} °C from 20 kg/s ({{dt_P20f}} MWe at {{dt_T20f}} °C in year 1).

Where the heat comes from decides the scale. Everything that touches a convecting or fractured reservoir reaches megawatts per well because it sweeps a large rock volume with the fluid; everything that relies on conduction through a borehole wall stays at about a megawatt of electricity or less per well whatever the depth (the 69 km Eavor-type loop reaches 1–2 MWe, the 20 km Quaise-type loop 1 MWe), because the rock around a hole can only pass a few hundred watts per metre once it has cooled. That is why the conventional and EGS figures in the source table are consistent with the model, while the closed-loop claims (2–9 MWe for a single retrofitted well, 8.2 MWe per Eavor loop, 100 MWe per Quaise plant) sit well above what the validated conduction model gives; the independent 2.2 MWe estimate quoted for the Eavor loop is of the right order. The cyclic single-fracture systems are conduction-limited between cycles in the same way: with the assumed 300 × 100 m fracture in 150 °C rock, twenty wells give {{dt_H13}} MWth and {{dt_P13}} MWe against the 50 MWe quoted; a fracture ten times larger in 200 °C rock would give roughly an order of magnitude more, still well short of it. Parasitics matter at the margin: a pumped medium-temperature well spends 10–20 % of its output on the pump, an EGS doublet 0.5–1 MWe on circulation against the fracture impedance, while self-flowing high-temperature wells and the deep closed loops (thermosiphon) spend almost nothing. Climate: the figures are for 15 °C ambient; with the GEOPHIRES availability taken at the actual ambient, the binary outputs fall by 30–55 % at 35 °C and 42–72 % at 45 °C (the cooler streams losing most), a 50 °C condenser costs the flash and steam plants 5–11 %, and direct heat is unaffected. The storage case recovers {{dt_st13}} % of the heat put into the fracture after a day, which is {{dt_se13}} % of the electricity once converted back; the SAGD chamber yields {{dt_rec17}} % of its stored heat in 15 years.

Verification against analytical solutions

Steady reverse circulation

With constant fluid properties and a fixed formation resistance, the down-annulus/up-tubing problem has a closed-form solution (the reverse-circulation form of Holmes & Swift 1970 and Kabir et al. 1996, re-derived and checked against a numerical boundary-value solve to 1e−13 K). The simulator converges to it at second order for insulated and bare tubing and for a 12 km well.

{{T_v1}} {{T_v2}}

Classical transient approximation

Kabir et al. (1996) combine the steady solution with the Hasan & Kabir dimensionless time function for the formation. It is an approximation (no vertical conduction, a fixed-flux time function), so agreement is expected to improve with time. The difference falls from 2 K at one day to 0.2 K at 30 years.

Field data

Four deep borehole heat exchanger tests have enough published data to attempt a re-run. Two are consistent with conduction from the published rock and match well. The other two report more heat than the rock could conduct even if the whole annulus stayed at the inlet temperature, so they cannot test a conduction model. The likely causes are groundwater flow or understated rock conductivity; the simulator was not tuned to them.

Benchmark models at depth

No field test reaches 4 km, so the deep range is checked against independent public models, each run with its own geometry and assumptions. The GeoCLUSTER database holds results from Sandia’s Sierra/Aria model, generated by the US DOE Closed-Loop Geothermal Working Group with IAPWS water properties; the comparison points are L-shaped wells (4–5 km vertical plus a 1 km lateral, which aharrsim also handles). SBT is the slender-body-theory model of Beckers et al., run from the public GeoCLUSTER code for vertical wells to 10 km.

Nalla et al. (2004/2005), TETRAD reservoir simulator used as a wellbore model, 5593 m, 350 °C at TD. Agreement is only moderate and follows a pattern: TETRAD’s outlet changes by just 2.6 K between k = 0.07 W/m/K insulation and perfect insulation, whereas in aharrsim, the analytical solution and the other benchmarks the riser heat loss matters far more. That makes TETRAD warmer at low flow, where the riser loss dominates, and cooler at high flow. At 31.5 kg/s aharrsim also includes the friction heating from about 30 MPa of pumping pressure.

{{T_nalla}}

Steam–water riser hydraulics

When the riser flashes, the pressure gradient model decides how much steam forms and where. It was tested on flowing pressure surveys of geothermal production wells compiled by Ambastha & Gudmundsson (1986), marching down from the shallowest measurement with the same correlations and IAPWS properties used in the simulator. For steam qualities up to about 0.35 the homogeneous model is within 2 %, better than the Orkiszewski correlation that performed best in the original study. At HGP-A, where the flow is 60 % steam by mass at 3–15 bar and close to choking, errors reach 7–31 %. A closed loop normally keeps the riser pressurised, which avoids that regime.

Not modelled

  • Groundwater flow or any convection in the rock; heat supply is pure conduction.
  • Temperature dependence of rock conductivity, which falls by roughly 20–35 % between 25 and 300–400 °C in crystalline rock. Rock layers can carry depth-dependent values; the 5 GW section shows the effect.
  • Heat capacity of the centre tubing wall, natural convection in a shut-in annulus, choked flow at the wellhead, and non-condensable gases.
  • Thermal bridges at vacuum-insulated tubing couplings: enter an apparent conductivity that includes them (vendor data are typically 0.01–0.06 W/m/K).
  • In the development-type examples: reservoir pressure decline and reinjection returns, scaling and corrosion, chemistry of supercritical fluids, the mechanics of fracture creation, and the number of flow paths an EGS actually makes; the fracture counts and areas are inputs.

Using the code

Python 3.9 or later with numpy, scipy, CoolProp, pyyaml and matplotlib. A case is a YAML file: survey or depth, casing and cement by interval, centre tubing layers, rock layers, geotherm, an operating schedule and the pressure boundary condition. examples/deep_8km.yaml documents every option.

pip install numpy scipy CoolProp pyyaml matplotlib
python -m aharrsim run examples/deep_8km.yaml --plot --progress

Results go to a folder next to the case: timeseries.csv (wellhead history), profiles.csv (depth profiles at the requested times), summary.json (outlet temperature, heat, exergy, steam fraction, pumping power, cumulative energy) and an optional summary.png. A 12 km, 30-year run takes about a minute on a laptop. The validation/ scripts regenerate every number on this page.

The other well types take the same YAML with mode: production | injection | utube, an inflow block (temperature or enthalpy, reservoir pressure, productivity index), an optional pump (depth, efficiency) and pressure_bc: outlet_MPa or bottom_MPa. Chains, reservoirs and plants are called from Python (aharrsim.systems, aharrsim.reservoir, aharrsim.plant); validation/dev_types.py holds one worked example of each of the twenty types and validation/verify_ext.py the benchmarks above.
